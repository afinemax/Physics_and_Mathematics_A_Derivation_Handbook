

\section{Maxwell's Equations}\label{sec:maxwell_equations}

Maxwell's equations in SI units are

\begin{subequations}\label{eq:maxwell_equations}
\begin{align}
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E &= \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0},
&& \text{(Gauss's law for electricity)}
\label{eq:gauss_electric}\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf B &= 0,
&& \text{(Gauss's law for magnetism)}
\label{eq:gauss_magnetic}\\
\nabla\times\mathbf E &= -\pdv{\mathbf B}{t},
&& \text{(Faraday's law of induction)}
\label{eq:faraday}\\
\nabla\times\mathbf B &= \mu_0\mathbf J
+ \mu_0\epsilon_0\pdv{\mathbf E}{t},
&& \text{(Ampère--Maxwell law)}
\label{eq:ampere_maxwell}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

where $\mathbf E$ is the electric field,
$\mathbf B$ is the magnetic field,
$\rho$ is the electric charge density,
$\mathbf J$ is the electric current density,
$\epsilon_0$ is the vacuum permittivity,
and $\mu_0$ is the vacuum permeability.

Another useful equation to remember is the
\textbf{continuity equation}, which expresses
the local conservation of electric charge:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\pdv{\rho}{t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf J = 0
}
\label{eq:continuity}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the electric charge density
and $\mathbf J$ is the electric current density,
defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf J = \rho\mathbf v,
\label{eq:current_density}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf v$ is the velocity of the moving
charge distribution. The SI units of $\mathbf J$
are $\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}$.
